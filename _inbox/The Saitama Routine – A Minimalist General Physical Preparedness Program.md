# The Saitama Routine – A Minimalist General Physical Preparedness Program

## 1. Training Philosophy

The purpose of this routine is to develop a well-rounded, physically capable body through a simple, sustainable daily training practice.

Inspired by the training philosophy of Saitama from _One Punch Man_, the program retains the appeal of daily conditioning, high-repetition calisthenics and consistency while making adjustments to improve muscular balance, movement quality and overall physical development.

The objective is not bodybuilding specialization or maximal strength in any single discipline. It is the development of general physical preparedness (GPP):

- Cardiovascular endurance and work capacity.
- Muscular strength and endurance.
- Functional mobility and flexibility.
- Trunk stability and body control.
- Balanced development of pushing, pulling, squatting and hinging movements.
- Resilience and the ability to perform physical work consistently.

The routine prioritizes simplicity, minimal equipment requirements, repeatable movements and progressive improvement.

**Central principle: Build a body that is strong, mobile, enduring and capable, rather than optimizing exclusively for appearance or performance in one exercise.**

## 2. Program Structure

The routine consists of four principal components:

1. Daily cardiovascular conditioning.
2. Isometric strength and mobility.
3. High-volume full-body calisthenics and resistance training.
4. Supplemental abdominal development.

The exercises are arranged to distribute muscular fatigue, balance opposing movement patterns and avoid unnecessary duplication.

---

## 3. Daily Cardiovascular Conditioning

**Exercise:** Running or rucking  
**Minimum distance:** 5 km daily.

Running and rucking provide the aerobic foundation of the program.

Running develops cardiovascular endurance and general locomotor capacity. Rucking introduces additional resistance through external loading, increasing the physical demands of walking and developing load-bearing work capacity.

Rucking also provides substantial lower-body and trunk stabilization demands.

The two modalities can be alternated according to preference, circumstances and recovery.

The minimum distance establishes consistency, but intensity should vary. Not every session needs to be strenuous.

---

## 4. Isometric Strength and Mobility

Performed following cardiovascular conditioning.

### 4.1 Antranik's Bodyline Drill

Purpose:

- Develop trunk strength and body control.
- Improve resistance to unwanted spinal extension.
- Develop lateral trunk stability.
- Establish control over body positioning.

The drill provides an integrated approach to isometric core conditioning.

### 4.2 Gymnastic Bridge

Purpose:

- Develop spinal and shoulder extension mobility.
- Improve shoulder extension capacity.
- Develop hip extension mobility.
- Strengthen the posterior chain in an extended position.
- Maintain familiarity and control in extended body positions.

The bridge complements the bodyline work by training movement and strength in a different range of motion.

Both exercises are retained as foundational components rather than replaced with redundant supplemental core exercises.

---

## 5. Strength and Muscular Endurance Circuit

The main strength circuit uses five movements covering the major fundamental movement patterns.

The default minimum is 50 repetitions per primary exercise, with lower repetition targets for more demanding strength movements.

### 5.1 Prisoner Squats

**Target: 50 repetitions**

Movement pattern: Squat.

Primary emphasis:

- Quadriceps.
- Glutes.
- General lower-body endurance.

The prisoner squat provides accessible, equipment-free lower-body training.

### 5.2 Prisoner Romanian Deadlifts

**Target: 50 repetitions**

Movement pattern: Hip hinge.

Primary emphasis:

- Hamstrings.
- Glutes.
- Spinal erectors.

The Romanian deadlift complements squatting by developing the posterior chain and hip-hinging capacity.

Progression can involve slower repetitions, increased range of motion or single-leg variations when the standard version becomes insufficiently challenging.

### 5.3 Elevated Push-ups

**Target: 50 repetitions**

Movement pattern: Horizontal push.

Primary emphasis:

- Chest.
- Triceps.
- Anterior deltoids.

Bar- or block-elevated push-ups provide a scalable pressing movement.

The elevation can be adjusted to accommodate strength and range-of-motion requirements.

### 5.4 Resistance Band Rows

**Target: 50 repetitions on Row-focused days.**

Movement pattern: Horizontal pull.

Primary emphasis:

- Rhomboids.
- Middle trapezius.
- Rear deltoids.
- Lats.

Rows provide a counterbalancing pulling movement to the push-ups.

A window-anchored resistance band can be used, with the anchor height adjusted to introduce different pulling angles.

### 5.5 Resistance Band Pulldowns

**Target: 50 repetitions on Pulldown-focused days.**

Movement pattern: Vertical pull.

Primary emphasis:

- Lats.
- Elbow flexors.
- Upper-back musculature.

Pulldowns complement horizontal rows by introducing a different pulling direction and shoulder movement pattern.

Rows and pulldowns alternate as the primary pulling exercise rather than necessarily being performed at full volume together.

### 5.6 Pike Push-ups

**Target: 15–25 repetitions.**

Movement pattern: Vertical push.

Primary emphasis:

- Deltoids.
- Triceps.
- Shoulder pressing strength.

Pike push-ups develop overhead pressing capacity without requiring external weights.

They are treated as a strength-oriented movement rather than another high-repetition endurance exercise.

Progression:

1. Incline pike push-ups.
2. Standard pike push-ups.
3. Feet-elevated pike push-ups.
4. Wall-supported handstand push-ups.

---

## 6. Upper-Body Exercise Organization

When performing the complete upper-body sequence, the preferred order is:

1. Elevated push-ups.
2. Resistance band rows.
3. Pike push-ups.
4. Resistance band pulldowns.

This alternates horizontal pushing, horizontal pulling, vertical pushing and vertical pulling.

The arrangement distributes muscular fatigue between opposing movement patterns and avoids performing consecutive pressing exercises.

### A/B Pulling Variation

To manage volume and avoid unnecessary overlap, the pulling movements can alternate:

|Exercise|Day A|Day B|
|---|---|---|
|Elevated push-ups|50|50|
|Band rows|50|—|
|Pike push-ups|15–25|15–25|
|Band pulldowns|—|50|

The rest of the routine remains unchanged.

When time permits, both pulling movements can also be incorporated at reduced individual volumes.

## 7. Supplemental Abdominal Development

The primary isometric trunk work is already provided by the Bodyline Drill.

Supplemental abdominal training therefore emphasizes dynamic abdominal strength and progressive hip-flexion control.

### Reverse Crunches

**Initial exercise and progression starting point.**

Purpose:

- Train active pelvic curling toward the rib cage.
- Develop abdominal strength through controlled trunk/pelvic flexion.
- Introduce dynamic abdominal work without requiring repeated upper-back flexion.

Progression:

1. Bent-knee reverse crunches.
2. Reverse crunches with knees progressively extended.
3. Reverse crunches with extended-leg lowering phases.
4. More demanding controlled leg-raise variations.

### Straight-Leg Raises

**Long-term progression target.**

Purpose:

- Develop hip-flexion strength.
- Challenge abdominal stabilization.
- Develop control over pelvic position while the legs move.

Progression should prioritize maintaining abdominal tension and controlling the lumbar spine rather than simply increasing repetitions.

Supplemental abdominal work is performed as time and recovery permit.

## 8. Additional Optional Exercises

These exercises are not mandatory components of the daily routine.

### Band Pull-aparts

Purpose:

- Rear-deltoid development.
- Upper-back endurance.
- Scapular control.

Suggested volume: 2 × 15–20 repetitions.

### Band External Rotations

Purpose:

- Rotator cuff strengthening.
- External rotation strength and shoulder control.

Suggested volume: 2 × 15 repetitions.

### Calf Raises and Tibialis Raises

Already substantially covered by occupational activity as a security guard.

Additional work is optional, particularly on days off.

## 9. Progression Principles

The program follows a simple progression hierarchy:

1. Consistency: Maintain the training habit.
2. Technique: Improve execution and movement control.
3. Range of motion: Gradually increase usable movement range.
4. Resistance: Increase loading or band tension where appropriate.
5. Difficulty: Progress to more demanding exercise variations.
6. Volume: Increase repetitions only when useful and recoverable.

The 50-repetition targets are minimum endurance-oriented benchmarks, not requirements to perform every repetition in one uninterrupted set.

Pike push-ups and other demanding strength exercises use lower repetition targets.

Progression should be gradual. The routine should not require maximal effort every day.

---

## 10. Recovery and Sustainability

Daily activity does not mean daily maximal intensity.

Running, rucking, occupational physical activity and high-volume resistance training all contribute to cumulative fatigue.

The program therefore allows:

- Alternation between harder and easier conditioning sessions.
- Adjustment of rucking loads.
- Reduced strength volume when recovery is insufficient.
- Easier days when occupational demands are high.
- Periodic reassessment of exercise difficulty and total workload.

Persistent joint pain, deteriorating performance or accumulating fatigue are reasons to adjust training rather than force completion of every target.

---

## 11. Guiding Principles and Intended Outcome

The routine is built around several guiding principles:

**Minimalism:** A small number of exercises should cover the major physical capabilities.

**Balance:** Opposing movement patterns should receive appropriate attention.

**Complementarity:** Exercises should contribute distinct benefits rather than duplicate the same stimulus unnecessarily.

**Progressive overload:** Improvement should come from better execution, increased difficulty and appropriate resistance.

**Mobility through strength:** Mobility should be developed alongside strength and control, not treated as an entirely separate pursuit.

**Sustainability:** The routine should be practical enough to maintain over years.

**General physical preparedness:** The ultimate objective is a body capable of running, carrying loads, lifting, pushing, pulling, stabilizing and moving comfortably.

The Saitama routine is therefore not simply a high-repetition challenge. It is a minimalist, full-body physical preparedness system built around daily conditioning, isometric control, fundamental movement patterns and progressive calisthenics.
