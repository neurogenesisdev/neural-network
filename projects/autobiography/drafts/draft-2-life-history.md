## Part I: Early Years (Birth to Age 5)

### Birth and Infancy

I was born on June 5, 1995, in Kiel, Germany. My birth story begins slightly earlier, when my German mother, Dorte Arp, and my American father, William Arthur Gordon, married in Laboe, Germany after learning they were expecting me. My mother took on the Gordon surname, becoming Dorte Gordon.

When I came into the world, my father was deployed on military duty in the United States and couldn't witness my birth. Instead, my mother was surrounded by her family—my German relatives—as she welcomed me, Luis William Gordon, into the world.

As a birth present, I received multiple stuffed animals. One in particular, gifted by my maternal grandmother, was Teddy—a white bear dressed in white overalls with a ribbon tie around his neck, roughly the size of my newborn self. Teddy quickly became deeply important to me, and I formed a strong attachment to him that would last throughout my childhood.

### First Home and Early Development

Shortly after my birth, we relocated to the United States, settling in El Paso, Texas, where we lived for approximately two years. From what my parents have told me, I was a remarkably quiet infant who rarely cried and was relatively easy to care for. During my infancy, I briefly battled meningitis but fortunately made a complete recovery.

It was in El Paso where my earliest personal memories begin to form. I recall playing with Legos, building castles, and completing puzzles with my father, as well as attending Gymboree play classes for toddlers. By all accounts, I was a bright, curious, and playful child. I formed a friendship with a girl my age who lived nearby.

One particularly significant memory from this time involves Teddy. After a day of shopping and running errands, we discovered that my beloved stuffed bear had gone missing. Uncharacteristically for my typically calm demeanor, I became inconsolable, distressed, and tearful. While my parents discussed the possibility of replacing Teddy, I insisted on finding the original, refusing to accept any substitute. We spent much of that afternoon retracing our steps through multiple stores before eventually finding him in the lost and found at a Target we had visited. My relief and joy were immense, and from that day forward, I never lost Teddy again. My mother used to fondly recount how I would pronounce the store name as "Targé" with a French accent, which she found both amusing and endearing.

### Family Expansion and Transitions

As our time in Texas continued, my mother became pregnant with my younger brother. Growing weary of life in the United States, my mother initiated another move—this time to Noblesville, Indiana, where my brother Jacob Gordon was born on November 12, 1997. I have clear memories of holding my infant brother, feeling proud and protective in my new role as an older sibling.

Our stay in Indiana was brief. Shortly after Jacob's birth, we returned to Germany, settling in Nußloch, near Heidelberg. Just as we were establishing ourselves there, my father received military deployment orders to Kosovo, where he would remain for several months until just before I was old enough to begin Kindergarten in the fall of 1998, when I was three years old.

### Early Challenges and Formative Experiences

During my father's deployment, my mother effectively became a single parent, caring for both me as a toddler and my infant brother. This period was particularly challenging for her as she faced multiple stressors simultaneously: managing two young children alone, living in a house that felt too confined for her comfort and was situated alongside a busy street, and coping with her lifelong genetic retinopathy that caused her vision to become increasingly impaired. The accumulation of these challenges weighed heavily on her during this formative time in our family's life.

Despite these difficulties, I have fond memories of playing in our backyard with a waterway play set, digging in the sandpit with shovels and buckets, racing Hot Wheels cars on my town-themed carpet, blowing soap bubbles, playing with superhero action figures and parachute toy soldiers, and of course, spending time with my beloved Teddy.

### First Encounters with Difficulty

My early years also contained some challenging experiences that would shape my understanding of the world. One minor incident occurred when I used the toilet by myself for one of the first times. I locked myself in the bathroom and couldn't figure out how to turn the key to unlock the door once I was finished—perhaps it was stuck somehow. I remember feeling increasingly distressed the longer I tried to unlock the door, eventually crying while my frustrated mother attempted to talk me through the process. She ultimately called a neighbor for help, who climbed through the bathroom window to rescue me.

A more traumatic memory from this period involved a punishment that went beyond what would have been reasonable for a child my age. While I don't recall exactly what behavior prompted my mother's frustration and anger, I vividly remember her decision to lock me in our dark, dank, dusty, and partially renovated basement. She turned off the lights before leaving and didn't tell me when she would return. I recall being dragged downstairs while crying and begging her not to do this, and I remember how cold, harsh, and unreceptive she was to my pleas. Alone in the basement, I was scared and confused, unable to find the light switch—or perhaps she had turned off the electrical power to the basement entirely. After crying for some time, I eventually resigned myself to waiting in the darkness until she returned. When she finally let me out, she seemed surprisingly regretful and apologetic about her actions, but having experienced this side of her, I was wary of accepting her attempts at consolation and remained emotionally distant for a while. This was my first encounter with my mother's emotional volatility and instability—behavior that would later prove to be not just an isolated incident but an integral part of her personality and a pattern throughout our lives.

Another significant memory from this time involves a neighbor's son who was approximately my age or slightly older. He was battling leukemia. While we didn't interact frequently except when passing by, I remember years later receiving the news that he had died from the disease. This represented my first confrontation with death—not within my immediate family, but of someone I knew nonetheless.

Not long after my father returned home, we put a down payment on a house in a nearby neighborhood of the same city and moved once again, addressing my mothers wishes to live in a more spacious house. The neighborhood was also home to significant number of US-American military families, which was great for the bilingual language development of my brother and I, and gave my father the chance to more easily communicate with the neighbors as he hadn't learnt much of the German language beyond the very basics. With the help of contractors, my father began the task of renovating the house which would take up a few months, but we quickly settled into our new home. On one of the first days of living there, after my brother and I had chosen our rooms and had finished exploring the house and garden we were encouraged by our parents to continue our exploration outside, and get to know the neighboring kids. With brother in step I left the house, wandered up the street and towards group of kids that were playing behind the fence in the garden of their house. Immediately I noticed that they seemed distrustful and hostile towards us, soon started to call us names and tell us to get lost. Although I was filled with adrenaline, instead of being intimidated and backing down I returned the favor and insulted them with what I thought was the worst curse word I knew and yelled to one of them: "You 'sud' of a bitch!" Not knowing what the expression meant except that it was bad and having apparently incorrectly learned that the it was supposed to be "son" and not "sud". I remember thinking to myself what they're problem was and what gave them to right to treat us like enemies despite knowing us at all. As if they had respected us as worthy challengers they immediately quieted down after my outburst, and I took my brother back inside. We didn't speak a word of it to our parents and the event was quickly put behind us. After this initial animosity we would soon encounter the neighboring kids again, this time on more amicable terms and slowly surely starting opening up and getting to know each other. There group of American kids were from three different families. There were the three siblings Ruben, Lucas, and Reina of which Ruben, a chubby, cheerful and gregarious boy, was the oldest, at about twice my age or more, followed by Lucas, a mischevious and lively boy, who was a bit older than me, and Reina, a reserved and shy girl, who was about my age. The family was of half native Hawaiian decent and eventually but quickly grew to become close friends. Then there was Jamey, about the same age as Lucas, a grumpy single child who owned a large dog that we jokingly called 'the humping hyena' much to his chagrin. And lastly there was a younger kid a bit younger than me called Ian, who was smaller, hyperactive, tended to annoy us, and didn't belong to what later became our core clique. For a few years, all throughout kindergarten, and until the early stages of elementary school Jacob and I spent a large part of our free time playing and enjoying our childhood as a raving band of rascals with Ruben as our leader being the eldest. We engaged in nerf gun, water gun and water balloon battles that spanned the neighborhood, stick fights, hide and seek, tag, shooting bow and arrows, building forts, and carving 'weapons', collecting Yu-Gi-Oh cards, imaginative play, and later video games on our Playstation 2, and Gameboy Advance. It was carefree endless fun and some of the best times of my life looking back.
