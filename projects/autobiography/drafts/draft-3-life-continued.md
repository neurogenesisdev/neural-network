## Part I: Early Years (Birth to Age 5)

### Birth and Infancy

I was born on June 5, 1995, in Kiel, Germany. My birth story begins slightly earlier, when my German mother, Dorte Arp, and my American father, William Arthur Gordon, married in Laboe, Germany after learning they were expecting me. My mother took on the Gordon surname, becoming Dorte Gordon.

When I came into the world, my father was deployed on military duty in the United States and couldn't witness my birth. Instead, my mother was surrounded by her family—my German relatives—as she welcomed me, Luis William Gordon, into the world.

As a birth present, I received multiple stuffed animals. One in particular, gifted by my maternal grandmother, was Teddy—a white bear dressed in white overalls with a ribbon tie around his neck, roughly the size of my newborn self. Teddy quickly became deeply important to me, and I formed a strong attachment to him that would last throughout my childhood.

### First Home and Early Development

Shortly after my birth, we relocated to the United States, settling in El Paso, Texas, where we lived for approximately two years. From what my parents have told me, I was a remarkably quiet infant who rarely cried and was relatively easy to care for. During my infancy, I briefly battled meningitis but fortunately made a complete recovery.

It was in El Paso where my earliest personal memories begin to form. I recall playing with Legos, building castles, and completing puzzles with my father, as well as attending Gymboree play classes for toddlers. By all accounts, I was a bright, curious, and playful child. I formed a friendship with a girl my age who lived nearby.

One particularly significant memory from this time involves Teddy. After a day of shopping and running errands, we discovered that my beloved stuffed bear had gone missing. Uncharacteristically for my typically calm demeanor, I became inconsolable, distressed, and tearful. While my parents discussed the possibility of replacing Teddy, I insisted on finding the original, refusing to accept any substitute. We spent much of that afternoon retracing our steps through multiple stores before eventually finding him in the lost and found at a Target we had visited. My relief and joy were immense, and from that day forward, I never lost Teddy again. My mother used to fondly recount how I would pronounce the store name as "Targé" with a French accent, which she found both amusing and endearing.

### Family Expansion and Transitions

As our time in Texas continued, my mother became pregnant with my younger brother. Growing weary of life in the United States, my mother initiated another move—this time to Noblesville, Indiana, where my brother Jacob Gordon was born on November 12, 1997. I have clear memories of holding my infant brother, feeling proud and protective in my new role as an older sibling.

Our stay in Indiana was brief. Shortly after Jacob's birth, we returned to Germany, settling in Nußloch, near Heidelberg. Just as we were establishing ourselves there, my father received military deployment orders to Kosovo, where he would remain for several months until just before I was old enough to begin Kindergarten in the fall of 1998, when I was three years old.

### Early Challenges and Formative Experiences

During my father's deployment, my mother effectively became a single parent, caring for both me as a toddler and my infant brother. This period was particularly challenging for her as she faced multiple stressors simultaneously: managing two young children alone, living in a house that felt too confined for her comfort and was situated alongside a busy street, and coping with her lifelong genetic retinopathy that caused her vision to become increasingly impaired. The accumulation of these challenges weighed heavily on her during this formative time in our family's life.

Despite these difficulties, I have fond memories of playing in our backyard with a waterway play set, digging in the sandpit with shovels and buckets, racing Hot Wheels cars on my town-themed carpet, blowing soap bubbles, playing with superhero action figures and parachute toy soldiers, and of course, spending time with my beloved Teddy.

### First Encounters with Difficulty

My early years also contained some challenging experiences that would shape my understanding of the world. One minor incident occurred when I used the toilet by myself for one of the first times. I locked myself in the bathroom and couldn't figure out how to turn the key to unlock the door once I was finished—perhaps it was stuck somehow. I remember feeling increasingly distressed the longer I tried to unlock the door, eventually crying while my frustrated mother attempted to talk me through the process. She ultimately called a neighbor for help, who climbed through the bathroom window to rescue me.

A more traumatic memory from this period involved a punishment that went beyond what would have been reasonable for a child my age. While I don't recall exactly what behavior prompted my mother's frustration and anger, I vividly remember her decision to lock me in our dark, dank, dusty, and partially renovated basement. She turned off the lights before leaving and didn't tell me when she would return. I recall being dragged downstairs while crying and begging her not to do this, and I remember how cold, harsh, and unreceptive she was to my pleas. Alone in the basement, I was scared and confused, unable to find the light switch—or perhaps she had turned off the electrical power to the basement entirely. After crying for some time, I eventually resigned myself to waiting in the darkness until she returned. When she finally let me out, she seemed surprisingly regretful and apologetic about her actions, but having experienced this side of her, I was wary of accepting her attempts at consolation and remained emotionally distant for a while. This was my first encounter with my mother's emotional volatility and instability—behavior that would later prove to be not just an isolated incident but an integral part of her personality and a pattern throughout our lives.

Another significant memory from this time involves a neighbor's son who was approximately my age or slightly older. He was battling leukemia. While we didn't interact frequently except when passing by, I remember years later receiving the news that he had died from the disease. This represented my first confrontation with death—not within my immediate family, but of someone I knew nonetheless.

### New Home and First Friendships

Not long after my father returned from Kosovo, we put a down payment on a house in a nearby neighborhood within Nußloch and moved once again, addressing my mother's wish to live in a more spacious home. The neighborhood was home to a significant number of American military families, which proved beneficial for the bilingual language development of my brother and me, while giving my father the opportunity to communicate more easily with neighbors, as he had learned only the basics of the German language. With the help of contractors, my father began renovating the house, a project that would take several months, though we quickly settled into our new surroundings.

On one of our first days there, after my brother and I had chosen our rooms and finished exploring the house and garden, our parents encouraged us to venture outside and get to know the neighboring children. With Jacob following me, I wandered up the street toward a group of kids playing behind the fence in their garden. Immediately, I sensed their distrust and hostility as they called us names and told us to get lost. Rather than being intimidated, I felt a surge of adrenaline and responded in kind, hurling what I believed was the worst insult I knew: "You 'sud' of a bitch!"—having incorrectly learned the expression and substituting "sud" for "son," with no real understanding of what the phrase meant beyond knowing it was offensive. I remember wondering what their problem was and why they felt entitled to treat us as enemies without knowing us at all. To my surprise, they quieted down after my outburst, as if they suddenly respected us as worthy challengers. I took my brother back inside, and we didn't mention the incident to our parents, quickly putting it behind us.

Despite this initial hostility, we soon encountered these neighboring children again on more amicable terms, gradually opening up and getting to know each other. The group of American kids came from three different families. There were three siblings: Ruben, Lucas, and Reina. Ruben, the oldest at about twice my age, was a chubby, cheerful, and gregarious boy who would eventually become our unofficial leader. Lucas, who was a bit older than me, was mischievous and lively. Reina, about my age, was reserved and shy. Their family was of half Native Hawaiian descent, and they quickly became close friends. Then there was Jamey, approximately Lucas's age, a grumpy only child who owned a large dog that we jokingly called "the humping hyena," much to his annoyance. Finally, there was Ian, slightly younger than me—a smaller, hyperactive child who tended to irritate us and never quite became part of our core group.

For several years, spanning kindergarten and into the early stages of elementary school, Jacob and I spent much of our free time playing with this band of neighborhood children, with Ruben serving as our leader by virtue of being the eldest. We engaged in Nerf gun battles, water gun and water balloon fights that spanned the neighborhood, stick fights, hide and seek, tag, archery, fort building, and whittling "weapons." We collected Yu-Gi-Oh cards, participated in imaginative play, and later enjoyed video games on our PlayStation 2 and Game Boy Advance. It was a period of carefree, endless fun—looking back, some of the best times of my life.

### First Steps into Education: Kindergarten

I vividly remember my first day of kindergarten. My father was again deployed to Kosovo, so my mother escorted me to school. I felt a mixture of excitement and apprehension about what awaited me, internally reluctant to enter but complying nonetheless. After being shown to my group room and introduced to my teachers, my mother wished me luck and left, leaving me alone in an unfamiliar environment.

During the first few hours after the welcoming ceremony and meeting the other children, I quietly retreated into one of the curtained nooks in a side room. I kept to myself, occupying my time with drawing, finding comfort in my own imagination and solitude as I gradually adjusted to this new environment. I suspect several factors contributed to why I wasn't my usual cheerful and curious self at this time. Prior to this, I hadn't had much practice speaking German with people outside my family and felt uncomfortable interacting with strangers in this language. Additionally, I was accustomed to engaging in play in less structured environments, or at least with self-imposed structure rather than under adult direction.

My demeanor changed dramatically when recess arrived and we were released into the expansive garden, complete with swings, hills, slides, tunnels, various obstacles, and numerous areas to explore. Without hesitation, I eagerly took advantage of these recreational opportunities and joined the other children in play. I naturally assumed the role of leader, directing what games to play next and establishing the rules of engagement. During this time, I met several children who would later become good friends, including Leonard, an imaginative boy who was perhaps slightly on the autism spectrum, and Julian, a fun-loving, chubby child.

There was also an older boy named Felix who had a reputation for being wild, rough, and somewhat of a bully. Though not on my first day, I eventually had a close encounter with him where I expected confrontation, but something in my demeanor seemed to earn his respect. He acknowledged me with a nod, and we occasionally played together afterward. This felt like a victory—the tough older kid had shown me respect and recognized me as an equal. I later shared this with my mother, though she didn't quite understand its significance and advised me to stay away from him.

Similarly, there was an Albanian boy named Franz who was aloof but very friendly and likely from a lower socioeconomic background. My mother also advised me not to socialize with him. Her dismissal of my newly found friend based solely on his social status sparked feelings of righteousness and indignation within me. I openly expressed my disapproval of her judgment and declared that I would continue to interact with him.

After recess, I returned to the classroom much more comfortable, actively and freely engaging in creative group projects like drawing and handicrafts, much to my teachers' surprise, as they had initially assessed me as shy and reserved. When my mother arrived to pick me up, I was in an inspired, cheerful mood, eager to share all that I had experienced that day.

Throughout my kindergarten years, or at least during the initial period, I continued this pattern of withdrawing for the first few hours of the day, then becoming wildly social during recess, and afterward embracing group activities with newfound confidence.

In kindergarten I discovered a passion for art, and began drawing pictures inspired by my imagination and things I saw in the world. I attempted copying the designs of trading cards, and fictional characters in media like anime characters as accurately as I could manage, and created my own characters that I imbue with abilities, weapons, names, and archetypal and narrative roles. On occasion I would integrate these characters and their depictions into my outdoor play, ascribing one of them to each of neighboring friends and we would roleplay using them as a basis for imaginative story oriented wars with handmade weapons. The skill based aspect of these activities combined with creative combined with the creative outlet it gave proved to be deeply fulfilling, but had a tendency to be overly critical of my work, not being satisfied with anything I deemed less than perfect to the point of tearing apart drawings that my parents deemed good and starting over. I was also very distraught at having my parents see my work before it was finished, and made it a habit to hide them from their sight until they were complete and would only then relish in their admiration and praise.

In these years we visited my maternal family in northern Germany multiple times, and I have fond memories of playing in my grandparents huge house and garden. My grandfather was a farmer and took me and my brother out on his farm, let us ride with him on his tractor, pet the horses. We would help him pluck berries, gather apples, and when were old enough let us shoot targets with his air rifle, which we proved to be skilled at and always looked forward to when visiting. My grandmother always cooked breakfast, lunch, dinner and baked for us, providing us with traditional German dishes, cakes, pies, and good heartful food all around. Often in the afternoon and evening everyone would just sit together in the living room by the fireplace while my grandma knit, the adults talked, my grandparents told stories, and my brother and I early played with each other or listened and I asked questions about what they were talking about. The rather strict parenting style of my mother would regularly lead to conflicts with mainly with my grandmother who scolded and pleaded with her to not be so harsh with us and overreact at the small ways we would annoy her simply by being active young boys. She never saw herself as being unreasonable, but would nonetheless give way to an extent for a limited period of time, which made only increased our affection for our grandma. When we were up in Laboe we also had lots of fun interacting with our cousins, especially the children of our aunt, Katrin, Lorenz, who is year younger than me, and Pauline who is about three years younger. 

I also had my first meaningful interactions with opposite sex at kindergarten. A few the girls expressed interest in me, in the sense of wanting to sit next to me during group activities and individual creative activity, and asking their parents to setup play dates with me. There was one time when at an organized sleepover at the kindergarten these two girls setup their sleeping bags to each of my sides because they wanted to be around me. At the time I didn't think too much of all this attention, as I usually focused on playing with the boys or pursuing my own interests, although I did enjoy their company. I do remember finding their preferred form of play rather incompatible with my own, too passive and inactive for my liking, and rather boring and stereotypically feminine. Where our interests did align was in arts and crafts which could engage in side by side and share and interact with each others work. On another occasion, on a class trip to the city hall while everyone was gathered on the town square, a third girl kissed my on lips, while I wasn't paying attention, which came as complete surprise to me. It seemed as if it was a dare or the other girls had hyped her to do it. I wasn't bothered by it though and remembering thinking about it for a while afterwards trying to figure out what to make of it. 

Regarding a completely different first experience during this time, there was one time when my mother accompanied my friend Julian and I to an outdoor swimming pool on warm summer afternoon. At a moment after we had finished playing, swimming, and were resting on our towels while asking about myself and my family whether or not my father was nigger then. My mother freaked out and scolded him for using that word, to which he responded with surprise, apparently not knowing that it was bad word and simply being curious. In terms appropriate for a child she then explained what it meant and cultural negative connotations associated with, which was novel information for me too at the time. This was the first time I experienced myself as racially and ethnically different from the people around me, and learned that other people might judge for my appearance to the point of wanting to hurt me with insults. Although, this incidence caused no distress in me we discussed the topic further later that day and it opened a pandoras box of new lenses through which to view the world and people.
